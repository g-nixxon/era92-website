Bidi Bidi is the second largest refugee settlement in the world. Roughly a quarter of a
million people, mostly from South Sudan, spread across hills that look quiet from the road
and aren&rsquo;t. We&rsquo;ve run a small Elevate cohort out of our Hub there for two years
now. This is what last week looked like.

## What&rsquo;s working

The simplest thing that&rsquo;s working: the cohort showed up. All sixteen. On time. With
notebooks. That&rsquo;s not a small thing in a place where any given day might include a
clinic visit, a long water-collection walk, or a sibling who needs supervision.

The work itself looked the way it usually does in week three of an Elevate cohort: people
discovering the difference between &ldquo;making something look nice&rdquo; and
&ldquo;making something work.&rdquo; A handful of moments worth writing down:

- One of the trainees, who arrived three years ago from Yei, redesigned a community board
  notice that her aunt runs. The new version is now in use. We are not above counting the
  small wins.
- Two trainees finished a brand-and-packaging concept for a soap-making collective in the
  neighboring settlement. The client wants to commission them when they graduate.
- A long conversation about what counts as a portfolio piece when the work serves your
  community more than it pads your CV. We did not resolve it. We are not trying to.

## What&rsquo;s harder than we expected

Three things, all of them mundane:

> Infrastructure is the curriculum. We can teach craft in the abstract; we can&rsquo;t teach
> craft when the wifi goes out for the seventh time on a Wednesday.

We expected the curriculum to be the hard part. It isn&rsquo;t. The hard parts are
electricity, bandwidth, and the cost of replacing the same charging cable for the twelfth
time. We budgeted for these. We did not budget for them right.

The second hard thing is sound &mdash; a small classroom inside a community center that runs
its own programs has a steady audio leak. Headphones help. We are looking for a better long-
term answer.

The third is more interesting. Several trainees are good enough at the work to leave the
settlement for paid roles. Some of them want to. Some of them want to stay. The model assumes
the former; the reality is at least half the latter, and we&rsquo;re re-architecting parts
of the placement pipeline to keep more of the work landing in Bidi Bidi itself.

## What last week taught us

The most concrete take-away: the next Hub buildout should treat power and bandwidth as
first-class budget lines, not facility line items. We&rsquo;ll publish the revised buildout
template once we&rsquo;ve done a second pass with the Hub team.

We&rsquo;ll be back here in three weeks. If you&rsquo;re reading this from a foundation
looking at refugee-settlement work specifically, we&rsquo;d love a conversation &mdash; the
infrastructure problem is the one we&rsquo;d most welcome partners on.
